## Methodology Review: Woodbury updates: solve the change instead of starting again

English · About 10 minutes

Two primary full texts and two publisher abstracts inspected. No raw numerical benchmark reproduction or browser visual QA.

### The intuition

Suppose a large linear system has already been factored, and the next task changes it in only one or a few directions. Starting the factorization again may discard most of the work. The Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury identity expresses the updated solution using solves with the original matrix and a smaller correction system. Low rank means few independent update directions, not necessarily few changed entries. [R1]

Imagine a shared nuisance pattern affecting every measurement. Its contribution can be dense across observations while depending on only one loading vector. That is the computational opportunity: represent the change compactly, then ask how the existing solution responds to it. The identity does not discover the nuisance pattern or certify that it should be removed. It only reorganizes the calculation once the matrices have been specified. Keep that distinction in mind whenever a fast linear-algebra trick acquires a biological interpretation.

### A small worked example

Use a deliberately tiny system so that every step can be checked. Let the original two-by-two matrix be diagonal with entries two and four, and let the right-hand side be the vector (2, 4). Solving gives (1, 1). Now add the outer product of the column vector u = (1, 2) with itself. The update matrix has rows (1, 2) and (2, 4), so the modified system matrix has rows (3, 2) and (2, 8). All four entries changed, but the added matrix has rank one.

Instead of immediately solving the new system, solve the old diagonal system with u as its right-hand side. The result is z = (0.5, 0.5). The inner product of u with z is 1.5, making the scalar correction denominator 2.5. The inner product of u with the original solution is three. Divide three by 2.5 to obtain 1.2, multiply z by that value, and subtract the result from the old solution. The new answer is (0.4, 0.4). Check it directly: the modified matrix times this vector gives (2, 4), the original right-hand side.

The inverse gives a second check, although building an inverse is unnecessary for the solve. The old inverse is diagonal with entries 0.5 and 0.25. The outer product of z with itself has every entry equal to 0.25. Dividing that matrix by 2.5 gives a correction whose entries are all 0.1. Subtracting gives inverse rows (0.4, −0.1) and (−0.1, 0.15). Multiplication by the modified matrix returns the identity. For a large system, the useful object would normally be the factorization and these solves, not a freshly materialized dense inverse.

There is a subtle bookkeeping trap when the update represents a new observation in least squares. Suppose the same vector u describes that observation and its response is one. The normal-equation matrix receives the outer-product update, but the right-hand side also gains u times one. It therefore changes from (2, 4) to (3, 6). Using the updated inverse with this new right-hand side gives (0.6, 0.6), not (0.4, 0.4). The earlier result was correct for a fixed right-hand side; it would be wrong for this different task. An efficient matrix update cannot compensate for an incorrectly specified problem.

A final toy case shows why the denominator matters. Start with the two-dimensional identity matrix. Use u = (1, 0) and a different vector v = (−1 + epsilon, 0), where epsilon is a small positive number. Adding the outer product of u and v produces a diagonal matrix with entries epsilon and one. The scalar correction denominator is epsilon. A right-hand side (1, 0) now has solution (1/epsilon, 0), which becomes very large as epsilon shrinks. At zero the system is singular. This behavior is not a failure of the identity: the updated problem itself has lost an informative direction.

### The necessary mathematics

Let A be an invertible n-by-n matrix, U and V be n-by-k update factors, and I_k the k-dimensional identity. Transpose is denoted by a superscript T. Define 

$$
B=A+UV^{\mathsf{T}},\qquad S=I_k+V^{\mathsf{T}}A^{-1}U.
$$

 If S is invertible, the updated matrix B is invertible and 

$$
B^{-1}=A^{-1}-A^{-1}US^{-1}V^{\mathsf{T}}A^{-1}.
$$

 For a right-hand side b, implement the identity through 

$$
Ay=b,\quad AZ=U,\quad Sq=V^{\mathsf{T}}y,\quad x=y-Zq.
$$

 Here y is the original solution, Z contains k original-system solves, q is the small-system solution, and x solves Bx = b. This formulation needs no inverse of U or V. [R1] [R2]

### Assumptions and failure modes

The saving requires a reusable solve with A and a correction dimension k small enough to make forming and solving S worthwhile. A low-rank update does not mean that its amplitude is small. Repeated changes also need not remain low rank when accumulated. Those are checks on the chosen representation and workload, not consequences of the identity itself. Before adopting the update route, compare its actual cost with refactorizing the matrix for your problem size.

Exact arithmetic and floating-point arithmetic are different questions. Yip’s publisher abstract gives a stability result about the existence of suitable update factors when the original and modified matrices are well conditioned. It does not promise stability for every factorization of the same update. Therefore, do not turn that result into blanket reassurance about arbitrary factors or repeated updates. Full proof-level conditions were not inspected for this reading. [R4]

As a proposed implementation check, compute the residual with the updated matrix, not just with the small correction system. Compare selected cases against a fresh solve and inspect sensitivity when the correction denominator or small system approaches singularity. A small residual alone does not guarantee a small solution error for an ill-conditioned problem. If the matrices come from least-squares normal equations, numerical conditioning already deserves attention before any update formula is introduced.

### Verified applications

Hager discusses applications including least-squares estimation and modifications of linear systems. The practical pattern is reuse of existing work after a low-rank change. These are documented numerical applications, not evidence that inverse updating is always the fastest or most accurate implementation for a particular omics workload. [R1]

Quiñonero-Candela and Rasmussen use the matrix inversion lemma in their treatment of sparse Gaussian-process regression. Their unifying account explains methods through the effective prior they employ. This gives an important distinction: the identity can evaluate a chosen low-rank model exactly while the chosen covariance remains an approximation to the original Gaussian process. Algebraic exactness should not be advertised as exact inference under an unchanged full model. [R3]

### Possible transfers — speculative

A possible omics use is sensitivity analysis for a regularized model when a small set of measured covariate directions is added. Cache a suitable base factorization, express the explicit matrix change through compact factors, and update all affected right-hand sides consistently. Compare predictions with a fresh fit on a manageable subset. This is a proposed engineering transfer, not a demonstrated biological result. Changing normalization, feature selection or the response model may alter the whole system, so those operations cannot automatically be treated as the same small update.

Another possible use is evaluating a diagonal-plus-low-rank covariance model for many observations. The smaller system can make repeated calculations feasible, but the scientific question remains whether the retained factors adequately describe dependence. Test predictions and uncertainty against a richer reference on held-out data. Faster covariance solves do not by themselves validate batch correction, recover causal effects or justify using a low-rank approximation in every tissue.

### What to try and what to read next

The useful habit is to separate what changed from what can be reused. Write the updated matrix and right-hand side explicitly, identify the correction rank, and verify the new solution against the actual updated equations. Only then ask whether the update route saves time. Start with the small worked example, then the primary reading’s computational organization; use the stability reading to resist treating an elegant algebraic identity as a numerical guarantee.
