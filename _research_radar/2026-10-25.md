## Methodology Review: D-optimal experimental design: buy complementary information, not more of the same

English · About 10 minutes

Historical thematic reading, not current news. Main and foundation inspected in full text; broader survey uses primary abstract evidence. Original worked example is illustrative; no omics implementation or empirical validation performed.

### The intuition

Suppose you can afford four measurements of a system with two unknown properties. A tempting plan is to repeat the easiest measurement four times. That can make one answer precise while leaving the other entirely unknown. Experimental design asks a different question from estimation: before observing the data, which measurements would make the eventual answers informative? The budget is fixed; the object being optimized is the arrangement of information, not the amount of data already collected.

Imagine uncertainty as an ellipse drawn around two parameter estimates. Repeating the same kind of measurement compresses the ellipse mainly in one direction. A complementary measurement compresses its neglected direction. D-optimality uses the volume of this joint uncertainty region as its target. This is an intuition, not a promise that every parameter improves equally. A thin but very long ellipse can have a modest area while still leaving one scientifically important combination poorly determined. [R1] [R2]

The main reading turns this geometry into a finite sensor-selection algorithm: a convex relaxation produces both a candidate selection and a performance bound. The final discrete selection remains a heuristic; the gap need not be small. Read it as a careful separation of what an optimizer certifies from what a rounded experimental plan actually achieves. [R1]

### A small worked example

Here is an original toy experiment, not an empirical omics result. Two unknown quantities are $$\theta_1$$ and $$\theta_2$$. A type-A measurement observes only the first, and a type-B measurement observes only the second. Each observation has independent Gaussian noise of variance one. Four observations are available, and repeated measurements are permitted. Let $$n_A$$ and $$n_B$$ be their respective counts, with $$n_A+n_B=4$$. If each count is positive, averaging within each type gives unbiased estimates whose variances are $$1/n_A$$ and $$1/n_B$$.

Compare two allocations. Three A measurements and one B measurement give variances one third and one. Two of each give one half and one half. The determinant of the covariance is therefore one third for the first plan and one quarter for the balanced plan. For these diagonal matrices it is simply the product of the two variances. Their uncertainty-ellipse area ratio, balanced divided by unbalanced, is $$\sqrt{3}/2\approx0.866$$. Thus balance reduces joint area by about thirteen percent, even though estimation of the first quantity becomes less precise.

Four A measurements are not merely a slightly worse plan: without a prior or another constraint, the second quantity is unidentifiable. Its uncertainty cannot be represented by a finite two-dimensional covariance ellipse. This distinction matters when implementing an optimizer. A singular information matrix should not be treated as an ordinary finite score. Adding a tiny numerical diagonal can make code run, but it also hides whether the data identify every parameter.

Now change the objective. If only the first quantity matters, spending everything on A may be sensible. The balanced answer was optimal for a joint two-parameter volume criterion, not for every possible scientific question. Likewise, if B measurements cost twice as much, the feasible allocations change. Write down the estimand, budget and permitted repetitions before optimizing. This example separates those choices so that a seemingly impressive determinant does not substitute for a scientific objective.

### The necessary mathematics

The standard linear model makes the geometry explicit. For measurement index $$i$$, write $$y_i=a_i^\top\theta+\epsilon_i$$. Here $$y_i$$ is the scalar observation, $$\theta$$ is the unknown parameter column vector, $$a_i$$ is its known sensitivity column vector, and the superscript $$\top$$ means transpose. The noise $$\epsilon_i$$ has mean zero and common variance $$\sigma^2$$, independently across measurements. For a selected index set $$S$$, define its information matrix $$M_S$$ by the following equation. If it is invertible, the least-squares estimator $$\hat\theta$$ has the stated covariance; $$\operatorname{Cov}$$ denotes covariance. 

$$
M_S=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{i\in S}a_i a_i^\top,\qquad \operatorname{Cov}(\hat\theta)=M_S^{-1}.
$$

 The inverse notation means matrix inverse, not elementwise reciprocals. Gaussian noise makes the usual fixed-confidence ellipsoid exact in this model with known noise variance. [R1]

In the toy allocation, the sensitivities are the two coordinate directions, so the information matrix is diagonal with entries $$n_A$$ and $$n_B$$. Its determinant is $$n_A n_B$$. Substituting $$n_B=4-n_A$$ gives $$n_A(4-n_A)=4-(n_A-2)^2$$, maximized at two. This direct calculation explains the balanced solution without relying on an optimization package. It instantiates the determinant criterion rather than proving that balanced allocation is generally optimal. [R2]

For continuous approximate designs, the classical equivalence theorem links determinant maximization to minimization of worst-case prediction variance over the design space. Its conditions and continuous design measures matter. It does not say that every fixed-size integer subset inherits the same optimality certificate. Keep the theorem, the finite allocation and the relaxation distinct: related mathematical objects can answer different operational questions. [R2]

### Assumptions and failure modes

The sensitivity vectors are part of the model, not discovered by the determinant. If a nonlinear response is approximated around the wrong parameter value, a locally informative design can be unhelpful for the real system. Before committing the entire budget, compare several plausible parameter settings and ask whether the chosen measurements change radically. This sensitivity analysis is a proposed diagnostic here, not a robustness guarantee supplied by the toy calculation. The broader survey motivates considering models beyond the linear setting; the suggested diagnostic is not a result verified from that abstract. [R3]

Independent measurement noise is also substantive. Technical repeats sharing a calibration error do not provide the information of independent replicates. In the toy example, a common offset affecting every B observation would not vanish merely by averaging more B observations. Similarly, an unmodeled batch effect can be precisely estimated as if it were the desired parameter. More precise model-based estimates can still be wrong. Include nuisance effects and the actual dependence structure before interpreting a design score. [R1]

The integer decision creates a second failure mode. Fractional allocation can ask for part of a sensor or a fraction of an experimental run. Rounding must restore a feasible plan and its information matrix must then be checked again. A good fractional score alone is not the quality of the executed experiment. Large numerical dynamic ranges are another warning: compare log determinants rather than multiplying many eigenvalues directly, and investigate near singularity rather than silently regularizing it away. [R1]

### Verified applications

Joshi and Boyd study selecting measurements for parameter estimation and evaluate their approach numerically. This is a verified computational sensor-selection application, not evidence of a deployed biomedical assay or a clinical benefit. Their paper supports the relaxation-based formulation and its explicit lack of a universally small optimality gap. No biological validation is claimed for the present worked example. [R1]

Huan, Jagalur and Marzouk survey classical, Bayesian and decision-theoretic design, including nonlinear and non-Gaussian settings and sequential policies that depend on previous outcomes. This establishes a broader computational context, not a claim that our linear determinant formula works unchanged in all those settings. Only the primary abstract and publication metadata are used for this related reading; its individual application studies have not been independently reassessed here. [R3]

### Possible transfers — speculative

A possible omics transfer would allocate a small pilot budget among perturbation conditions whose predicted responses depend on a few explicitly chosen model parameters. This is speculative. A condition with a large response is not automatically useful if it repeats the same parameter sensitivity as conditions already chosen. One could compare a determinant-based plan with random selection and a simple biologically motivated baseline, while reserving independent validation conditions. The cited sensor paper does not validate this proposal.

The experiment would first need credible sensitivities, a count or transformed-data observation model, and donor-level replication. Thousands of cells from one donor should not be substituted for thousands of independent experimental units. Include required controls and minimum donor coverage as constraints, not optional rewards. If the scientific question concerns a single contrast, optimize that contrast rather than all model coefficients indiscriminately. If the model cannot make defensible response predictions, stop at a pilot study instead of presenting a computed design as authoritative.

### What to try and what to read next

Try the four-measurement example by enumerating every allocation. Mark singular plans explicitly, calculate both individual variances and joint area, then change either measurement cost or the scientific target. This small exercise reveals what the objective rewards and what it ignores. For a real project, record the feasible experiments, independent unit, noise assumptions, uncertainty target and validation plan before selecting an algorithm.

Read the main sensor-selection paper for the bridge from geometry to a computable plan, the equivalence paper for the precise classical theorem, and the modern survey for questions that outgrow the linear model. The practical lesson is not to maximize a determinant whenever possible. It is to spend scarce measurements on information the current design lacks, while keeping model error, feasibility and the actual scientific decision visible.

  Main reading: study the measurement model and relaxed subset-selection problem before the numerical experiments. The author-hosted PDF is the journal article, not a separate paper.

- [R2] **Related reading:** [The Equivalence of Two Extremum Problems](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/canadian-journal-of-mathematics/article/equivalence-of-two-extremum-problems/B8B0626C11F52B0FD8C67C5D54BDDD43). J. Kiefer and J. Wolfowitz (1960).
  Foundational theorem: understand why determinant optimality and worst-case prediction variance coincide for approximate designs under the stated regression assumptions, not arbitrary finite subsets.
