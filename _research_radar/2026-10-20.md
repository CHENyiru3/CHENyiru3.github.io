## Methodology Review: ADMM: let simple subproblems negotiate a shared answer

English · About 10 minutes

Classic methodological reading; primary sources support the stated algorithm and one expression-data application. Proposed transfers are explicitly speculative; optimization success does not establish biological validity.

### The intuition

Imagine two collaborators revising one numerical answer. One cares about fitting the measurements; the other wants a simple, structured answer. If each edits the same number without remembering earlier disagreements, they can keep undoing each other. A more useful arrangement gives each collaborator a working copy and keeps a running account of their disagreement. This is a teaching analogy: the collaborators are mathematical subproblems, and their shared answer is a parameter vector. The question is whether a complicated fit can be assembled from operations that are easy to solve separately.

The alternating direction method of multipliers, or ADMM, makes that arrangement precise. It introduces copies of a variable, solves two penalized subproblems in turn, then updates a multiplier that carries information about disagreement forward. A quadratic penalty encourages agreement during each update. The multiplier is what prevents this from being merely two independent fits with a fixed compromise penalty. This is an optimization method: it finds a solution to the model you specify. Whether that model captures the biology remains a separate question. [R1] [R2]

### A small worked example

Consider a deliberately artificial one-number fit. A measurement equals three, and we want a fitted value $$w$$ that balances squared error against an absolute-value penalty. Define the objective $$F(w)=\tfrac12(w-3)^2+\lvert w\rvert$$. The penalty coefficient is one. For positive $$w$$, its derivative is $$w-2$$, so the candidate minimum is two; the negative branch decreases toward zero, whose objective is larger. Thus the unique optimum is $$w=2$$ and $$F(2)=2.5$$. This known answer lets us audit an iterative solver rather than trusting its output. [R2]

Make two scalar copies, $$x$$ for the squared-error term and $$z$$ for the absolute-value term, and require $$x=z$$. Use penalty parameter $$\rho=1$$ and start the copy $$z^0=0$$ and scaled multiplier $$u^0=0$$; superscripts count iterations. In this example the updates simplify to $$x^{k+1}=(3+z^k-u^k)/2$$, then $$z^{k+1}=S_1(x^{k+1}+u^k)$$, then $$u^{k+1}=u^k+x^{k+1}-z^{k+1}$$. Here $$k$$ is the current iteration and $$S_1(v)=\max(v-1,0)-\max(-v-1,0)$$ shrinks the input $$v$$ toward zero by one, stopping at zero. [R1] [R2]

The first update gives $$x^1=1.5$$, $$z^1=0.5$$ and $$u^1=1$$. The two copies disagree by one. Substituting them into the split objective gives $$\tfrac12(1.5-3)^2+0.5=1.625$$, which is below the true minimum of 2.5. Nothing miraculous happened: this pair violates the equality constraint, so it is not an eligible solution to the original problem. Evaluating the original objective at the available copy instead gives $$F(0.5)=3.625$$. A low number beside an infeasible iterate can therefore be misleading. These values are direct arithmetic for this example. [R1]

The next round gives $$x^2=(3+0.5-1)/2=1.25$$, $$z^2=S_1(2.25)=1.25$$ and $$u^2=1$$. Agreement is now exact, but the answer is still wrong: $$F(1.25)=2.78125$$. The third round moves both copies to 1.625. Thereafter the multiplier stays at one and each new copy is the midpoint between the preceding copy and two, so the sequence approaches the known optimum. This small counterexample to a careless stopping rule is the main lesson: agreement between copies at one iteration does not establish that the optimization has finished. [R1]

### The necessary mathematics

For the general consensus form, let $$x$$ and $$z$$ be vectors with the same number of coordinates, and let $$f$$ and $$g$$ be the two objective functions. We minimize $$f(x)+g(z)$$ subject to $$x=z$$. Fix a positive scalar $$\rho$$ and let $$u$$ be the equality-constraint multiplier divided by $$\rho$$. With iteration index $$k$$, scaled ADMM is 

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{k+1}&=\operatorname{argmin}_x\left(f(x)+\frac{\rho}{2}\lVert x-z^k+u^k\rVert_2^2\right),\\
z^{k+1}&=\operatorname{argmin}_z\left(g(z)+\frac{\rho}{2}\lVert x^{k+1}-z+u^k\rVert_2^2\right),\\
u^{k+1}&=u^k+x^{k+1}-z^{k+1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

 The argmin selects the minimizing vector, and the squared Euclidean norm adds the squared coordinate differences. Each minimization is a proximal step: improve one objective while paying for movement away from the other copy, shifted by the multiplier. [R1] [R2]

For this particular equality constraint, define the primal residual $$r^k=x^k-z^k$$ and dual residual $$s^k=\rho(z^{k-1}-z^k)$$. The first measures disagreement; the second measures a remaining optimality mismatch through the change in the second copy. Stopping requires both residual norms to be small, using absolute and relative tolerances appropriate to the scale. In our scalar example, iteration two has $$r^2=0$$ but $$\lvert s^2\rvert=0.75$$. The second diagnostic catches precisely what agreement alone missed. Residual formulas change for more general linear constraints. [R1]

### Assumptions and failure modes

The standard convex guarantee assumes two closed, proper, convex functions and an unaugmented Lagrangian saddle point, with the subproblems solved. Proper excludes an objective that is impossible everywhere or takes negative infinity; closed rules out missing limiting boundary values. The saddle-point condition ensures a compatible primal optimum and dual multiplier. With fixed positive penalty, the basic result gives feasibility, objective-value and dual convergence; it should not be paraphrased as unconditional convergence of every individual variable in every linear-constraint formulation. [R1]

Splitting into three convenient sequential blocks is not an innocent implementation change. Chen and colleagues exhibit convex examples where the direct multi-block extension diverges. Their result does not condemn all multi-block methods; it says that the two-block guarantee cannot simply be inherited. Likewise, inserting a nonconvex neural-network component requires its own analysis. Before adapting a published solver, match the actual update order, objective assumptions and constraint structure to the theorem being invoked. [R4]

For a practical audit, keep model tuning and solver tuning in separate columns. Changing the absolute-value penalty in our example changes the requested answer. Changing the algorithmic penalty changes how the copies negotiate. Test the implementation on the known scalar solution, then on a small problem solved independently. Record tolerances and inspect both residual traces. These are proposed engineering checks, not a claim that passing a toy test validates a biological analysis.

### Verified applications

A verified omics example is the joint graphical lasso of Danaher, Wang and Witten. It estimates related Gaussian precision matrices across classes, using penalties to encourage sparse networks and selected forms of similarity. Their algorithm separates matrix likelihood updates from structured-penalty updates using ADMM. The inspected author manuscript applies the fused formulation to airway-epithelial microarray measurements from patients with lung cancer and controls. This is a demonstrated use of the optimizer in an expression-data analysis, not an application invented from the scalar example. [R3]

The application also shows why a solver and a scientific conclusion must be evaluated separately. The authors describe the network analysis as visualization and hypothesis generation, with preprocessing and tuning choices shaping the result. Under the Gaussian model, precision-matrix zeros encode conditional independence; estimated edges do not by themselves demonstrate causal regulation. The evidence here verifies the reported application and computational construction. It does not establish universal accuracy, causal discovery, or superiority over present-day network methods. [R3]

### Possible transfers — speculative

A possible exercise is to fit one predefined response from a small donor-level expression matrix while combining a data-fitting loss with an explicitly chosen coefficient penalty. This is a speculative teaching transfer, not a validated workflow from the application paper. First decide what a row represents, why the loss matches that response, and how penalty strength will be chosen without using the evaluation data. Implement a two-copy split only if its separate updates are genuinely easier than solving the original problem. Compare the objective and coefficients with an independent solver before considering a larger dataset.

A second experiment could compare several computational implementations of the same fixed model on simulated expression-like data. Hold preprocessing and model penalties fixed; vary only solver tolerances and the algorithmic penalty. Ask whether predictions or selected coefficients change when numerical accuracy is tightened. This would investigate computational sensitivity, not biological reproducibility. If two implementations disagree, investigate their objective scaling, convergence diagnostics and intercept handling before concluding that one method has found a different biological pattern.

### What to try and what to read next

Try reproducing the first three scalar iterations with a calculator. Keep three separate records: the two copies, the original objective evaluated at a single candidate value, and both residuals. The conspicuously low first split objective and the premature agreement in the second round make useful unit tests. Then change the starting copy and see whether the answer remains two. The goal is to understand exactly what the solver is certifying, rather than to memorize an acronym.

For further reading, use the main survey for the algorithm and stopping rules, the proximal survey for individual updates, the joint graphical-lasso paper for a concrete expression-data use, and the multi-block counterexample for the limits of casual modification. The practical question to carry forward is simple: which parts of my objective become easier when separated, and what will certify that their answers have actually reconciled? [R1] [R2] [R3] [R4]
