## Methodology Review: Savitzky–Golay filters: preserve local shape and estimate a slope

English · About 10 minutes

Four original sources and author software documentation freshly checked; exact toy calculation verified. No published spectra or benchmarks reanalyzed.

### The intuition

Imagine five readings from a detector near the shoulder of a broad peak. There is a slow bend in the underlying trace and fast variation from measurement noise. The simple moving average is a reasonable way to suppress some of the fast variation, but its one number has no notion of slope or curvature. A short fitted curve can retain either feature. A Savitzky–Golay filter fits a low-degree polynomial to each small window and returns the fitted value or derivative at the middle. It estimates a local shape rather than demanding that the entire trace be polynomial. [R1] [R2]

At interior points on a uniform grid, there is a computational shortcut. Each window has the same offsets from its middle, and the least-squares coefficients depend linearly on the measured values. One set of fitted weights can therefore be applied at every interior location. The filter is a fixed weighted sum even though the polynomial it represents changes from window to window. The apparent simplicity of that sum hides a modeling choice: select the shapes the fit can reproduce before deciding how much noise it should remove. [R1] [R2]

### A small worked example

Consider five invented readings 4, 8, 10, 10 and 8 at integer offsets minus two through two. They come from a quadratic with intercept 10, slope one per index and quadratic coefficient minus one. In physical coordinates let the sample spacing be one half of a unit. A five-point average reports 8 at the middle. A quadratic fit reports 10 there, because the five values lie exactly on the fitted curve. It also reports a slope of two per physical unit at the middle. This is the slope at the sample we chose, not a claim that the sample sits exactly at the peak.

Now raise only the rightmost reading by five, as though that sensor reading were corrupted. After refitting, the center falls from 10 to 67/7, about 9.57, while the estimated slope rises from two to four per physical unit. A positive error can lower the fitted center because the outer weights in the center estimate are negative. The change in slope is larger at that outer location than it would be for the same perturbation at the center, where the slope weight is zero. These opposite effects make it worthwhile to check the level and derivative separately.

### The necessary mathematics

Let $$j$$ denote the index offset and $$y_j$$ the observation there. Fit the curve $$a+bj+cj^2$$ by unweighted least squares. The intercept estimate and the derivative at the center, written in physical units when $$h$$ is the grid spacing, are 

$$
\hat a=\sum_{j=-2}^{2}\frac{17-5j^2}{35}y_j,\qquad \hat d=\frac{1}{10h}\sum_{j=-2}^{2}j y_j.
$$

 The role of $$h$$ is just to change units: the curve was fitted against sample index, not the physical coordinate. Its derivative in index units is the fitted linear coefficient, since the quadratic term has zero derivative at the center. [R1] [R2]

To derive the weights, the odd powers of the symmetric index offsets sum to zero. The squared offsets sum to ten and the fourth powers to 34; the normal equations for the intercept and quadratic coefficient are 

$$
5a+10c=\sum_{j=-2}^{2} y_j,\qquad 10a+34c=\sum_{j=-2}^{2}j^2y_j.
$$

 Eliminating the quadratic coefficient gives the first formula. The independent equation for the slope is ten times the linear coefficient equals the sum of the index-weighted readings. The five center weights are minus 3, 12, 17, 12 and minus 3, divided by 35. They reproduce constants, linear trends and quadratics at the center because their zeroth moment is one and their first and second moments are zero. [R1] [R2]

### Assumptions and failure modes

The guarantee belongs to the specified evenly spaced, centered window with unweighted least squares. Fitting a polynomial at irregular physical positions is possible, but the offsets change and these particular weights no longer apply. At a boundary, copying, reflecting or extrapolating measurements changes the effective operator. The first few values of a processed trace should not be treated like interior points. A centered window also uses readings from after its middle and cannot be treated as a real-time forecasting rule. [R1] [R3]

For independent, equal-variance observation errors, the five center weights retain 17/35 of the observation-error variance, whereas the moving average retains 1/5. The fitted curve preserves a larger class of local shapes but passes more of that simple noise. Strong preservation near zero frequency is also compatible with weak attenuation at unwanted high frequencies. Schmid and colleagues illustrate boundary artifacts and weak rejection above the passband, particularly troublesome for estimating derivatives. Accuracy on a quadratic does not protect a sharp jump, a very narrow peak, or a noisily estimated slope. [R2] [R3]

### Verified applications

Gibb and Strimmer show smoothing as one preprocessing stage for MALDI mass spectra, alongside baseline correction and peak detection. Their package documentation explicitly offers a Savitzky–Golay method and a moving average. Schmid and colleagues use an experimental infrared spectrum to illustrate weak noise suppression and the difficulty of higher derivatives. Taken together, these are a documented instrument-data application and a documented limit. They do not establish that every apparent peak or shoulder is chemical signal, or that changing the smoother alone improves identification in an arbitrary assay. [R3] [R4]

### Possible transfers — speculative

A possible diagnostic for any uniformly sampled assay trace is to decide which feature matters, then compare its stability across a raw trace and a few plausible preprocessing choices. Height, integrated area, location and slope are different targets. Use technical replicates or synthetic traces with a known signal, and examine boundaries separately. This is a suggested transfer, not a report of successful validation on a new measurement. Adjacent filtered points share input observations; apparent smoothness does not add independent evidence or resolve confounding.

If slope is the target, repeat the comparison after changing the sampling interval rather than merely changing the number of points. A five-point window spans four intervals, so a fixed window in points covers a different physical span after resampling. This matters when a scientifically meaningful feature has a known characteristic width. Keep window span in physical units in the analysis record; otherwise two nominally identical filtering settings can operate at different scales.

### What to try and what to read next

Try the five-point example twice: first reproduce the level and slope on the exact quadratic, then move one error across the window. The first exercise tells you which shapes the filter protects; the second tells you how it can mislead. For a real trace, keep the raw values, record the grid and window, and compare methods by the measurement feature you need rather than by visual smoothness alone. [R1] [R3]

### Reading list

- [R1] **Primary reading:** [Smoothing and Differentiation of Data by Simplified Least Squares Procedures](https://doi.org/10.1021/ac60214a047). Abraham Savitzky and Marcel J. E. Golay (1964).
  Read the local fit and numerical differentiation sections. Derive the center weights from the normal equations rather than relying on a printed coefficient table.

- [R2] **Related reading:** [Smoothing by Savitzky-Golay and Legendre Filters](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-0-387-21696-6_11). Per-Olof Persson and Gilbert Strang (2003).
  Read the centered-window derivation and impulse response. A polynomial fit, its value at one point, and its frequency response are three views of one operation.

- [R3] **Related reading:** [Why and How Savitzky–Golay Filters Should Be Replaced](https://doi.org/10.1021/acsmeasuresciau.1c00054). Michael Schmid, David Rath and Ulrike Diebold (2022).
  Compare the failure modes before choosing a filter. This paper demonstrates shortcomings rather than proving that one replacement is optimal for all measurements.

- [R4] **Related reading:** [MALDIquant: a versatile R package for the analysis of mass spectrometry data](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/bts447). Sebastian Gibb and Korbinian Strimmer (2012).
  Read Figure 1 and the smoothIntensity documentation. The filter is a preprocessing option, not an identification or inference procedure.
