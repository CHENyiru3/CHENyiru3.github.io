## Methodology Review: Persistent homology: which holes survive a change of scale?

English · About 10 minutes

Primary tutorial full text inspected; foundations journal abstract only; stability theorem checked in the 2005 conference manuscript alongside 2006 online journal metadata. No biological benchmark reproduced, and proposed omics uses are speculative.

### The intuition

A scatterplot can suggest a ring, several islands, or a filled cloud. But a single plotting scale can be misleading. Connect nearby observations using a very small distance and almost everything is isolated. Increase the distance enough and almost everything is connected. Persistent homology asks which topological features appear and disappear while that distance changes. Its output records intervals of survival rather than insisting that one neighborhood radius is the uniquely correct choice. [R1]

The abstract problem is geometric: summarize connectivity and holes in a finite set of observations. A hole here is a property of a constructed space, not automatically a missing cell type, a regulatory feedback loop, or a physical cavity. Imagine a photograph of people standing around a fountain. Their positions may outline a loop, but those positions alone cannot tell you why the people stood there. Keep that separation between a measured shape and its explanation throughout the analysis.

### A small worked example

Take four points at the corners of a unit square: $$(0,0)$$, $$(1,0)$$, $$(1,1)$$ and $$(0,1)$$. Call the distance threshold $$r$$. Our rule is explicit: include an edge whenever the Euclidean distance between its endpoints is at most $$r$$, and fill every triangle whose three edges are present. More generally, include every higher-dimensional simplex whose edges are present. This is the Vietoris–Rips convention used in this example; the threshold is an edge length, not a ball radius.

For $$0\leq r\lt1$$ there are four isolated vertices and no loop. At $$r=1$$ all four sides enter together. The square becomes one connected component with one unfilled loop. No triangle is present because each possible triangle needs a diagonal. At $$r=\sqrt{2}$$ both diagonals appear, together with all triangles and the three-dimensional simplex on the four vertices. The loop is filled and disappears. Thus the one-dimensional barcode has one interval $$[1,\sqrt{2})$$, with lifetime $$\sqrt{2}-1\approx0.414$$. The interval includes its birth but excludes its death under our convention.

The zero-dimensional barcode tracks components separately. All four components are born at zero; three terminate when the sides connect them at one, while one component persists indefinitely in ordinary, unreduced homology. Simultaneous merges can make the choice of representative component arbitrary, but they do not change these interval endpoints. The square example is an original hand calculation for this review, not a result from an omics dataset. Its purpose is to make the filtration rule and the difference between components and loops concrete.

Now change the units so every coordinate is multiplied by ten. The loop still has the same qualitative shape, but its birth and death distances both become ten times larger. Its raw lifetime also increases tenfold. Nothing biological has become ten times more convincing. This simple modification shows why a barcode must travel with its distance definition and preprocessing. For another exercise, add the square’s center as a fifth point and determine when triangles enter. You should no longer expect the original loop interval to survive unchanged.

### The necessary mathematics

Let $$K_r$$ denote the simplicial complex at threshold $$r$$. Increasing the threshold gives nested complexes: $$K_r\subseteq K_s$$ whenever $$r\leq s$$, where $$s$$ is a second threshold. Fix a coefficient field, for example arithmetic modulo two. The vector space $$H_k(K_r)$$ records independent homology classes in dimension $$k$$; $$k=0$$ concerns components and $$k=1$$ concerns loops. Its dimension $$\beta_k(r)=\dim H_k(K_r)$$ is the Betti number. The inclusion maps between complexes also induce maps between these vector spaces. Persistent homology retains those maps, not just a separate count at each threshold. For finite one-parameter filtrations over a field, interval summaries have a rigorous algebraic basis. [R1] [R2]

A separate theorem concerns sublevel sets of continuous tame functions $$f$$ and $$g$$ on the same triangulable space. Tameness imposes finite-dimensional homology and finitely many homological changes. Writing $$D(f)$$ and $$D(g)$$ for their persistence diagrams, the theorem states: 

$$
d_B(D(f),D(g))\leq\lVert f-g\rVert_\infty.
$$

 Here $$d_B$$ is the bottleneck distance: the smallest worst matching displacement between diagram points, allowing matches to the diagonal. The norm on the right is the largest absolute difference between function values. It bounds how far the summaries move under this specified perturbation; it does not certify a biological interpretation. [R3]

### Assumptions and failure modes

Distance is a scientific choice. If one gene’s numerical scale dominates the representation, nearby cells may mean little more than similar values of that gene. If an embedding erases a feature before the topology is computed, persistence cannot recover it. Conversely, a convincing ring in a two-dimensional visualization need not be a ring in the original representation. For an exploratory analysis, compare a prespecified expression representation with the visualization rather than silently treating the latter as ground truth.

Sampling also matters. A missing region can arise because a state was not sampled, because quality control removed it, or because the system avoids it. A long interval alone does not distinguish these possibilities and is not a p-value. Donor imbalance, batch structure, and correlated observations remain experimental-design problems. Decide in advance how biological replicates support inference. Running the same geometry calculation on more cells from one donor does not create more independent donors.

Computation has a further practical boundary: even a modest number of vertices can generate many higher-dimensional simplices. Start with a small, explicitly bounded problem and report any subsampling or maximum dimension. These choices affect what you can conclude. Failure to compute a feature because the construction was truncated should not be reported as evidence that the feature is absent.

### Verified applications

The inspected stability paper develops uses in recovering the homology of sampled sets and comparing geometric shapes. These are established mathematical applications under stated assumptions. Otter and colleagues also benchmark persistent-homology implementations on synthetic and empirical inputs. Their study is useful for understanding the computational pipeline, but its historical benchmark does not identify the best software in 2026. This review has not reproduced those calculations or validated an omics classifier. [R1] [R3]

### Possible transfers — speculative

A possible omics experiment would ask whether a predefined cell-state representation contains a reproducible cyclic organization across independently collected donors. This is a speculative transfer, not a claim that the cited papers establish a cell-cycle detector. Choose features without using the desired topological answer, freeze normalization, and compare equally sized donor-specific samples. Inspect whether a putative cycle remains when known technical covariates are controlled. Use independent biological information only afterward to test an interpretation.

A second speculative use is as an audit of a learned representation. Compare a carefully chosen reference geometry with the geometry after compression, while keeping samples and distance conventions explicit. If a loop appears only after aggressive compression, that is a question to investigate, not an automatic discovery. If a feature disappears, inspect which observations and neighborhoods changed before blaming either representation. The useful outcome may simply be a clearer account of what information the embedding preserved or discarded.

### What to try and what to read next

Start by drawing the four-point square and writing down exactly which vertices, edges, and filled triangles exist at each threshold. Check both component and loop intervals. Then change one assumption at a time: units, one point’s position, or the addition of a central point. You should be able to explain every altered endpoint before processing thousands of observations. Read the main tutorial for the pipeline, the algebra paper for why interval summaries exist, and the stability paper for the precise scope of robustness. The most useful question is not merely whether a hole appears, but which choices and evidence make that hole scientifically interpretable.
