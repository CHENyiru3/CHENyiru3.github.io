## Methodology Review: Empirical Bayes shrinkage: letting precision decide how much to trust an effect

English · About 10 minutes

Historical primary research, not a new-paper alert. Full text was inspected in the identified Stephens author manuscript, Smyth’s corrected author PDF, and the apeglm publisher article. Benchmarks were not rerun; the worked example is original and illustrative. Online dates and later journal-issue dates are distinguished in the reading notes.

### The intuition

Imagine a screen reporting thousands of changes, each accompanied by an uncertainty estimate. Two genes both show a two-unit increase, but one estimate is precise and the other is mostly noise. Sorting by the observed changes treats them as equally persuasive. Empirical Bayes shrinkage instead asks what the whole collection teaches us about plausible effects, then combines that information with the precision of each measurement. A noisy estimate moves farther toward the shared center; a precise estimate retains more of its original value. The goal is better estimation across the collection, not proof that every shrunken estimate is closer to its unknown truth. [R1]

The empirical part is learning a distribution of effects from the ensemble. The Bayes part is updating that distribution with each feature’s own evidence. Features need not have identical effects: they share a model for how effects vary. This is useful whenever many related quantities are measured with unequal precision. It also forces a scientific choice: which features reasonably belong in the same ensemble, and what would make zero a sensible center? A prior is an assumption about that collection, not a declaration that biology should be uninteresting. [R1]

### A small worked example

Consider an original toy screen measured in log-base-two fold-change units. Gene A has estimate 2 and standard error 0.5; gene B also has estimate 2 but standard error 2. For transparent arithmetic, suppose an ensemble fit has supplied a zero-centered normal prior with variance 1. We hold that fitted prior fixed. This illustrates the final updating step of empirical Bayes, not a claim that two genes suffice to learn a trustworthy prior. The normal prior is a teaching simplification of the more flexible distributions in adaptive shrinkage. [R1]

Under this model, multiply each estimate by the prior variance divided by the sum of prior and measurement variances. A keeps 1 divided by 1.25, or 80 percent, giving 1.6. B keeps 1 divided by 5, or 20 percent, giving 0.4. The identical raw estimates now lead to different posterior means because their evidential weight differs. Their conditional posterior standard deviations are about 0.447 and 0.894 respectively. B remains substantially less certain, even after its conspicuous raw effect has been pulled inward. These numbers are direct calculations, not observed experimental results. [R1]

Change the assumed prior variance from 1 to 4. A now keeps 4 divided by 4.25, giving about 1.882; B keeps one half, giving 1. With a broader population of plausible effects, both are shrunk less. This sensitivity is the point of fitting the prior rather than choosing an arbitrary penalty. Now imagine an observed effect estimate of 10 with standard error 0.5: the first Gaussian model still multiplies that estimate by 0.8. Its simplicity can become a liability for unusually large signals. That motivates checking flexible or heavy-tailed alternatives, rather than treating shrinkage as an automatic improvement. [R1] [R3]

### The necessary mathematics

Let j index a feature, let $$\beta_j$$ be its unknown effect, let $$y_j$$ be its observed effect estimate, and let $$s_j$$ be the known positive standard error used in this simplified calculation. Write $$N(a,b)$$ for a normal distribution with mean a and variance b. The model is $$y_j\mid\beta_j\sim N(\beta_j,s_j^2)$$ and $$\beta_j\sim N(0,\tau^2)$$, where $$\tau^2$$ describes between-feature variation. Completing the square in the product of the likelihood and prior gives posterior mean $$m_j$$ and variance $$v_j$$: 

$$
\begin{aligned}
m_j&=w_jy_j,\qquad w_j=\frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2+s_j^2},\\
v_j&=\frac{\tau^2s_j^2}{\tau^2+s_j^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

 Here $$w_j$$ is the fraction of the original estimate retained. These conditional formulas produce the worked example; in practice, both the standard errors and prior parameters may themselves be estimated. [R1]

How can the ensemble identify the prior? In this Gaussian model, an estimate has marginal variance $$\tau^2+s_j^2$$. The spread of observed estimates therefore combines biological effect variation and measurement noise. Simply using that observed spread as the prior variance counts the noise twice. Adaptive shrinkage, implemented in ashr, fits a richer unimodal distribution using a mixture of components with fixed scales and learned weights. Its normal-mixture version includes zero-centered normals and optionally a point mass at zero. Likelihood-based fitting accounts for each standard error; the paper also uses a penalty favoring conservative estimation of the zero component. [R1]

An effect estimate and a significance measure answer different questions. For directional confidence, Stephens defines the local false sign rate as the smaller of the posterior probabilities that the effect is nonpositive and nonnegative. Both events include an exactly zero effect, so a point mass at zero must be counted. This is a posterior error probability under the fitted model, not an ordinary p-value. A small directional error probability also does not establish a biologically substantial effect: an extremely precise but tiny change can have a well-determined sign. [R1]

### Assumptions and failure modes

First, precision estimates must be credible. A small reported standard error makes a feature resist shrinkage; if that error is underestimated, the procedure can confidently preserve noise. The primary paper explicitly discusses unstable standard errors with few samples and the limitations of normal or t likelihood approximations. More features do not repair a biased experimental contrast. Batch effects or unmeasured confounding can shift many estimates together, violating the working independence assumptions and distorting the fitted effect distribution. Shrinkage should follow a defensible measurement and experimental-design model. [R1]

Second, the pooling population matters. A zero-centered unimodal prior may poorly describe several distinct biological response classes. The simplest adaptive-shrinkage model also assumes effects have the same distribution regardless of their standard errors; Stephens discusses extensions when effect magnitude and standard error are related. Check sensitivity to sensible groupings and distributional choices rather than selecting whichever produces the most discoveries. Fitting the prior and then treating it as fixed also omits uncertainty about that fitted prior. Conditional credible intervals should not be advertised as universally calibrated intervals. [R1]

Third, distinguish what is being shrunk. limma’s moderated t-statistic stabilizes residual variance estimates toward a shared estimate; that operation does not itself shrink the fitted contrast in its numerator. Effect-size shrinkage changes the estimated contrast instead. They can coexist, but are not interchangeable. In the Gaussian toy model a single precision-dependent multiplier affects even extreme observations. apeglm addresses this tail behavior with an adaptive Cauchy prior and a count-data likelihood, while still relying on adequate normalization, model fit, and posterior approximation. [R2] [R3]

### Verified applications

Smyth’s microarray paper provides a concrete variance-moderation application. In the four-array zebrafish Swirl experiment, the moderated approach ranked both BMP2 control probes first and both Dlx3 probes next; the ordinary t-statistic and raw fold-change ranking did not reproduce that ordering. The paper also analyzes an ApoAI knockout experiment. These are inspected examples of empirical Bayes variance estimation inside a designed comparison, not evidence that raw expression values should be pooled across unrelated samples. [R2]

For effect-size shrinkage, Zhu and colleagues evaluate apeglm on a highly replicated yeast RNA-seq dataset, repeatedly taking small subsets and comparing their estimates with a full-dataset reference. Heavy-tailed shrinkage improved effect-size ranking relative to unshrunk estimators in that setting and avoided some excessive shrinkage of large effects from the normal-prior comparator. The same paper reports cell-line-mixture benchmarks with different trade-offs. The full-data reference is an estimate, and the results do not establish a winner for every dataset, metric, or present-day software release. [R3]

### Possible transfers — speculative

A speculative transfer would estimate perturbation effects on a prespecified panel of proteomic features, then compare unshrunk estimates with precision-aware shrinkage. Fit the measurement model at the level of independent biological samples, retain the original estimates, and assess predictions on held-out replicates. Predefine whether success means lower squared estimation error, more stable rankings, or better direction recovery; these objectives can disagree. Check whether missing measurements, abundance-dependent noise, or distinct protein classes undermine a common prior. This is a proposed validation experiment, not an application established by the three readings.

### What to try and what to read next

The practical lesson is to preserve both an effect estimate and its uncertainty. Reproduce the two-gene calculation, vary the prior spread, and explain which estimates move most. Then ask whether the ensemble is scientifically coherent and whether its uncertainty estimates are trustworthy. Read Stephens for the general strategy, Smyth for the separate problem of noisy variance estimates, and Zhu and colleagues for the cost of thin prior tails. A useful result is not merely a tidier plot: it is a defensible improvement on a stated estimation task, with the original evidence and the sensitivity to pooling still visible. [R1] [R2] [R3]
