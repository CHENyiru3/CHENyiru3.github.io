Methodology Review: Partial regression: why removing a nuisance variable from the outcome is not enough

English · About 10 minutes

Primary sources and mathematical example checked; no experimental reproduction.

The intuition

Suppose you want to compare an exposure with a measured response, but both change with age. A tempting shortcut is to subtract the age trend from the response and regress what remains on the original exposure. The response now has no linear association with age, so surely age is gone? Not quite. The exposure still contains variation aligned with age. That variation contributes to its spread even though it cannot explain the age-residual response. The resulting slope can be smaller simply because its numerator and denominator describe different comparisons.

Partial regression repairs that mismatch. Remove the same nuisance predictors from both the response and the exposure, then compare the two residuals. The Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem says that this yields exactly the exposure coefficient from one ordinary least-squares model containing the exposure and all nuisance predictors. This is a finite-sample algebraic identity, not an asymptotic approximation or a causal guarantee. Its practical value is making the meaning of an adjusted coefficient visible: it uses exposure variation that the chosen nuisance model cannot explain. [R1]

A small worked example

Consider four invented observations from two batches. The batch indicator is zero for the first two observations and one for the last two. Exposure values are 0, 2, 2 and 4; response values are 2, 4, 7 and 13. Fit a response model with an intercept, batch and exposure. Direct least squares gives intercept 1, batch coefficient 3 and exposure coefficient 2. The fitted responses are 1, 5, 8 and 12, leaving residuals 1, minus 1, minus 1 and 1. These are synthetic numbers chosen to expose the algebra, not a biological experiment.

Regressing on an intercept and this batch indicator amounts to subtracting each batch mean. Exposure means are 1 and 3, so residual exposures are minus 1, 1, minus 1 and 1. Response means are 3 and 10, so residual responses are minus 1, 1, minus 3 and 3. Their cross-product sum is 8 and the residual-exposure square sum is 4. The residual-on-residual slope is therefore 2, exactly matching the joint model. Its fitted residual-response vector is minus 2, 2, minus 2 and 2; subtracting that vector leaves the same four errors as before.

Now make the tempting mistake: regress the batch-residual response on the original exposure, with an intercept. The centered original exposure is minus 2, 0, 0 and 2. Its cross-product with the residual response is still 8, but its square sum is now 8. The slope becomes 1. Nothing biological changed. Keeping between-batch exposure variation in the denominator halved the estimate. If exposure were constant within each batch, residual exposure would instead be all zeros: no separate exposure coefficient could be identified after adjusting for batch.

The necessary mathematics

Let y be the n-vector of responses, x the n-vector of one exposure, and Z an n-by-k nuisance design matrix including an intercept. Assume Z has independent columns and x adds an independent direction. The identity matrix I has dimension n. Define the residual-making matrix and the two residual vectors by

\[M_Z=I-Z(Z^{\mathsf{T}}Z)^{-1}Z^{\mathsf{T}},\qquad \tilde{x}=M_Zx,\qquad \tilde{y}=M_Zy.\]

The transpose symbol turns columns into rows. This matrix is symmetric and idempotent: applying the same residualization twice makes no further change. It annihilates every column of Z. [R1]

In the full model, a is the k-vector of nuisance coefficients, b is the exposure coefficient and e is the residual vector. The least-squares estimate satisfies

\[\hat{b}=(\tilde{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{x})^{-1}\tilde{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{y}.\]

One way to see why is to fix b temporarily. The best nuisance fit removes from y minus xb everything explained by Z. What remains is the residual response minus b times the residual exposure. Minimizing its squared length over b gives the displayed expression. The resulting residuals equal those of the full model, not merely its exposure coefficient. [R1]

The outcome-only shortcut instead uses the centered original exposure, denoted x_c. With an intercept in Z, its slope relates to the correct slope through

\[b_{\mathrm{shortcut}}=\hat{b}\frac{\tilde{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\tilde{x}}{x_c^{\mathsf{T}}x_c}.\]

The ratio lies between zero and one whenever both denominators are positive. In the example it is one half. This exact statement concerns one exposure and the same unweighted linear projection; it is not a universal claim about every multivariable or nonlinear adjustment scheme. [R1] [R3]

Assumptions and failure modes

The nuisance design is part of the question, not disposable bookkeeping. An intercept plus a linear age term removes a straight-line age relationship; it does not remove an arbitrary curved trend. Including age squared, interactions or batch indicators defines a different projection and potentially a different adjusted comparison. Use the same observations, missing-data exclusions and nuisance columns for both residualizations. For weighted least squares, the projection and residual comparison must use compatible weights. Replacing either nuisance regression with a random forest does not preserve this exact finite-sample theorem.

Identifiability can deteriorate before it disappears. If batch almost predicts exposure, little residual exposure remains and the coefficient becomes sensitive to noise. This is a lack of informative within-batch contrast, not a numerical inconvenience that residualization cures. Nor does an adjusted slope become causal merely because nuisance effects were projected out. Which variables should be adjusted for depends on the scientific design; conditioning on a mediator or collider may answer the wrong question.

Matching coefficients does not mean every software standard error matches. The full toy model uses three fitted coefficients and leaves one residual degree of freedom. A naïve no-intercept residual regression counts only one fitted coefficient and reports three residual degrees of freedom. With residual square sum 4, its conventional variance estimate for the slope is one third rather than the correct 1. For the same observations, Ding establishes exact equality for unadjusted HC0 robust covariance, but not automatic equality for leverage-adjusted HC2 or HC3. Inferential validity still requires appropriate sampling assumptions. [R1]

Verified applications

Lovell studied seasonal adjustment as a regression problem: seasonal nuisance components can be represented by regression columns rather than treated as a separate, opaque preprocessing step. His original discussion also warns that regressions on adjusted series can overstate significance without the appropriate correction. This historical application motivates the distinction between getting an adjusted coefficient right and accounting for nuisance fitting when estimating uncertainty. [R2]

Krieger and colleagues examined age adjustment in epigenetic-clock studies using a literature review, simulations and empirical analyses. They showed that treating an age-residual outcome as automatically age-adjusted can attenuate exposure associations; adjusting for age in the analysis or using the corresponding joint model avoids that particular mismatch. Their example makes the theorem biologically consequential without implying that a statistical clock residual is itself a causal measure of aging. [R3]

Possible transfers — speculative

A useful prospective omics diagnostic would compare a joint linear model with a residualized implementation on exactly the same samples and nuisance design. Disagreement beyond numerical tolerance would flag different centering, covariates, exclusions or weights. This is an implementation check, not validation of the biological adjustment set. For count data, a log-normalized linear analysis and a negative-binomial model have different objectives; this identity cannot certify that one reproduces the other.

For a perturbation experiment spread across plates, plotting residual response against residual perturbation coding could reveal whether the data contain enough within-plate treatment contrast, or whether almost all original treatment variation is between plates and removed by adjustment. The diagnostic would not recover a treatment contrast if every plate contains only one treatment. Before fitting increasingly flexible corrections, inspect whether the experiment actually supplies the comparison the coefficient is meant to describe.

What to try and what to read next

Residualization is not a magic cleaning operation. It is a precisely specified projection that changes which variation remains available for comparison. For a linear adjusted effect, remove the same nuisance space from both sides, retain the full model’s uncertainty accounting, and inspect how much exposure variation survives. If almost none remains, the honest conclusion is weak identification, not successful removal of confounding.
