Methodology Review: Markov-chain lumpability: when a smaller state space keeps the same predictions

English · About 10 minutes

Original theorem chapter and three full primary papers read; toy calculations checked, no benchmark or raw-data reproduction.

The intuition

Imagine a technician who records whether a machine is in room A or room B, but cannot see its setting inside room A. Suppose there are two settings. If either setting gives the same chance of entering B at the next observation, knowing the hidden setting adds nothing to that particular prediction. But if one setting nearly always leaves and the other almost never does, the label A hides consequential information. The history of room visits may then indicate which setting the machine is in, even though the current room does not. A smaller state description can create apparent memory in a system that originally had none.

Strong lumpability gives an exact test for when this loss of detail is harmless for predictions about rooms. It does not ask whether two settings look alike, or whether they move among the hidden settings in the same way. It asks whether their total transition probabilities into each observed room agree. This distinction matters whenever a graph or dynamic model is summarized by clusters. A cluster can be visually coherent and internally sticky while hiding two very different ways to leave. Conversely, hidden states can behave very differently inside a block and still make identical predictions about the next block. [R1]

A small worked example

Consider an invented discrete-time chain on hidden states \(a_1,a_2,b\). Record both \(a_1\) and \(a_2\) as room A, and \(b\) as room B. In that order for both rows and columns, let the transition matrix be \(P=\begin{pmatrix}1/2&1/4&1/4\\1/8&5/8&1/4\\1/2&0&1/2\end{pmatrix}\). Its first row means that from the first setting in A, the next observation stays there half the time, moves to the other setting one quarter of the time, and enters B one quarter of the time. The second setting has a different internal pattern but the same one-quarter chance of entering B.

At room level the matrix is therefore \(Q=\begin{pmatrix}3/4&1/4\\1/2&1/2\end{pmatrix}\). Starting in A, there are two ways to end up in B after two observations: remain in A first and then leave, or enter B first and then stay there. Their probabilities sum to \((3/4)(1/4)+(1/4)(1/2)=5/16\). This is the same whether the hidden start is the first or second setting. The aggregate model preserves this prediction, but not the probability of ending at a specific setting within A.

Now replace the transitions by a deterministic cycle: the first A setting goes to the second, the second goes to B, and B returns to the first. In a stationary run, a present A following B must be the first setting, so the next room is A with certainty. A present A following A must be the second setting, so the next room is B with certainty. The current room is identical in the two cases; the previous room changes the prediction. Averaging over both settings would give a one-step escape probability of one half, but it would erase the pattern that reveals the hidden phase.

The necessary mathematics

Let \(X_t\) denote the microstate at integer time \(t\), and let \(P_{xy}=\Pr(X_{t+1}=y\mid X_t=x)\) be the fixed transition probability from microstate \(x\) to microstate \(y\). Partition the microstates into disjoint blocks \(C_1,\ldots,C_k\), where \(k\) is the number of blocks, and record only the block of the current state. For any starting block \(C_i\) and target block \(C_j\), the row-sum criterion requires that \(\sum_{y\in C_j}P_{xy}=\sum_{y\in C_j}P_{x^{\prime}y}\) whenever \(x,x^{\prime}\in C_i\). The common value is the macro-transition entry \(Q_{ij}\). The sums compare whole blocks, not the entries within them. [R1]

The proof is short. Conditional on any observed history ending in block \(C_i\), the microstate is a mixture over that block. At the microstate level, the Markov assumption says that the future depends on the current microstate, not its past. Averaging a constant next-block probability over the mixture gives the same constant, regardless of the weights. Conversely, initialize the chain separately at two states in the same block. If a single macro-transition matrix works for every initial distribution, its first row of predictions cannot differ across those two starts. The resulting exact model therefore preserves every finite sequence probability of observed block visits for a given initial block distribution. [R1]

Assumptions and failure modes

The statement assumes a finite, time-homogeneous Markov chain, a fixed partition, and the chosen discrete observation interval. A model estimated at a different lag or under a different intervention has a different transition matrix to check. Failure of the criterion means there is no common transition matrix valid for every microstate initialization. It does not rule out Markov behavior for particular initial distributions; that weaker possibility has a separate definition. Nor does passing the criterion certify an empirically fitted microstate model. Exact lumpability of a wrong model remains exact only inside that model. [R1]

Sampling matters when the matrix is fitted rather than known. Two rarely observed states may have very similar empirical transition rows just because neither has been observed leaving. Conversely, small noisy row differences need not reflect a meaningful failure at the forecast horizon. Compare direct estimates of multi-step block transition probabilities with iterated one-step predictions on held-out trajectories when possible. A discrepancy is a warning; close agreement at a few lags is not a proof that the projection is Markov under all initializations. The test also cannot identify the mechanism of a transition. [R1] [R3]

Verified applications

In molecular simulation, Bowman builds a hierarchy of coarser Markov state models by comparing microstates with finite-sampling uncertainty in mind. The article analyzes molecular dynamics-derived models of alanine dipeptide and villin, and compares candidate partitions with other methods using Bayes factors. That is a demonstrated setting in which dynamic, rather than purely geometric, similarity is useful. It is not a demonstration that the resulting molecular macrostates satisfy exact lumpability, or that the reported comparison transfers to other data types. [R3]

Network studies make another distinction concrete. E and colleagues choose an approximation objective for a random walk and study when it recovers lumpable partitions. Piccardi uses stationary-weighted, one-step block retention to score network communities. One-step retention is meaningful for the specified walk and weighting even when the observed block sequence is not itself Markov. In both cases the outputs are properties of the chosen model of movement on a graph, not evidence that nodes physically move or that communities represent biological mechanisms. [R2] [R4]

Possible transfers — speculative

A possible omics exercise is to ask whether a coarse cell-state label makes a good forecast for a genuinely longitudinal or lineage-tracked dataset. At one defined interval and condition, estimate the distribution of next coarse labels separately from each finer starting state. Compare those distributions across fine states within a coarse label, preserve uncertainty for rare transitions, and evaluate held-out biological replicates. Destructive snapshots, pseudotime, or a neighborhood graph alone do not supply observed transitions. One can still audit lumpability of a learned graph walk, but that result would concern the walk, not measured cell-state kinetics.

What to try and what to read next

Start with two questions: what detail am I dropping, and what future event must remain predictable? Add each transition row over the proposed destination blocks and compare rows within each starting block. If they match, use the smaller chain for predictions about blocks. If they differ, retain the relevant hidden distinction or make an explicitly approximate forecast and test it. Reproducing both toy chains by hand makes the key distinction clear: a good state label is one that keeps the information needed for the prediction, not merely one that makes the diagram smaller. [R1]
