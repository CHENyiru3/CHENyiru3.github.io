What biological maps preserve

A larger dataset does not automatically provide new evidence about development, anatomy, or treatment response. Today’s atlas studies measure different kinds of variation, and the methodology review explains when a smaller representation can preserve a precisely defined quantity: pairwise distance.

Methodology Review: Random projections: preserving distances with fewer coordinates

English · About 10 minutes

Four original publications inspected. Exact toy distances verified; published benchmarks were not reproduced.

The intuition

Suppose the only thing you need from thousands of molecular features is a useful estimate of the distance between two observations. Keeping only the first few features can miss the very feature that makes the observations different. A random projection takes another route. Every output coordinate is a signed mixture of the input coordinates. A difference that disappears in one mixture may show up in another. You trade exact coordinates for approximate distances, then ask how many mixtures are enough for the comparisons you care about.

The Johnson–Lindenstrauss result is surprising because the number of required coordinates grows logarithmically with the number of points, for fixed distortion and confidence, rather than directly with the original dimension. The guarantee concerns the pairwise distances of a specified finite set, not all directions in the ambient space. It does not claim that genes are interchangeable, or that a mixed coordinate has an interpretable molecular meaning. The quantities being preserved are distances in the representation already chosen. [R1] [R2]

A small worked example

Consider three invented points: A is (0, 0, 0), B is (1, 1, 0), and C is (1, 0, 1). Each of the three pairwise squared Euclidean distances equals two. Choose two signed sums and divide each by the square root of two. The particular sign matrix used here is

\[S=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&-1\\1&-1&1\end{pmatrix}.\]

A maps to zero. B gives unscaled sums two and zero, while C gives zero and two. After scaling, A-to-B and A-to-C each retain squared distance two, but B-to-C has squared distance four. A single map can be accurate for some pairs and inaccurate for others.

Now consider the difference of B and C, namely (0, 1, minus 1). In one independently drawn sign row the last two signs agree half the time. Their contributions then cancel, giving squared output zero. Otherwise the output is plus or minus two, giving squared output four. With two independent rows and the prescribed scaling, the projected squared distance is zero, two, or four with probabilities one quarter, one half, and one quarter. Its mean is exactly two, but that is only the average over possible maps. The same pair even collapses completely one quarter of the time at this deliberately tiny target dimension.

The necessary mathematics

Let \(x\) and \(y\) be two points with \(d\) input coordinates, and let \(v=x-y\) be their difference. The map \(R=S/\sqrt{k}\) uses a matrix \(S\) of \(k\) rows and \(d\) columns whose entries are independently chosen from plus and minus one with equal probability. The Euclidean norm is denoted by \(\lVert\cdot\rVert_2\), and \(\mathbb{E}\) means averaging over draws of the signs. Then

\[\mathbb{E}\lVert Rv\rVert_2^2=\lVert v\rVert_2^2.\]

To see why, square a signed sum of the coordinates of the difference. Each diagonal term retains its squared coordinate because a squared sign is one. Every cross term has mean zero because the two signs are independent and have mean zero. The scaling makes the final squared norm the average of the squared sums, rather than their total. [R1]

The mean alone says nothing about whether a particular map is accurate. The second part of the argument bounds the chance of a large deviation for one fixed difference vector, then counts all pairs that might fail. If the set contains \(n\) points, the number of unordered pairs is \(n(n-1)/2\). A union bound adds the failure probabilities across those pairs; the failures need not be independent. A sufficient target dimension scales as \(\epsilon^{-2}\log(n/\delta)\) up to constants for this construction, for relative squared-distance tolerance \(0<\epsilon<1\) and target failure probability \(0<\delta<1\). In this statement the same random map is applied to every point. This is a finite-set guarantee, not a guarantee for points chosen after seeing the map. [R1] [R3]

Assumptions and failure modes

The input metric matters. If preprocessing lets one feature dominate Euclidean distance, preserving the distance faithfully preserves that dominance. The theorem neither selects a normalization nor makes irrelevant differences disappear. It also does not guarantee that two close competitors remain in the same order: their allowed distance ranges may overlap. A theoretical dimension bound can exceed the original number of coordinates when tolerance is tight. In that case the bound supplies no argument for compression, even if an empirical check might justify a smaller dimension for a narrower objective. [R1] [R3]

Using one fixed map is essential for comparability. Independently redrawing a matrix for each observation does not preserve their differences by the argument above. Also distinguish a dense sign map from a sparse map. A dense output may consume more memory than a sparse input, and making the sign matrix sparse by arbitrary zeroing is not covered by the dense construction. Achlioptas analyzes a particular sparse alternative. Measure memory and compute separately from distortion, and avoid concluding that one advantage automatically entails the others. [R1]

Verified applications

Wan and colleagues used ensembles of sparse random projections in SHARP, a single-cell RNA-seq clustering pipeline. They assessed both preservation of cell-to-cell distances and agreement with reference cell labels in reported benchmarks. Their procedure also includes dataset partitioning and the combination of multiple clusterings. The results therefore support an actual use of random projection in transcriptomics, but they do not isolate the projection as the source of every gain or establish a universal advantage over PCA. Their sparse implementation and pipeline are not identical to the dense sign example above. [R4]

Possible transfers — speculative

One possible atlas exercise is to compare candidate nearest neighbors produced by a random sketch with exact neighbors in a fixed reference representation. Start with a modest held-out subset and measure both pairwise distortion and neighbor recall. Report those checks separately for abundant and rare groups. If global distances look stable but rare-state neighbors change, increase the target dimension or use exact distances to rerank a shortlist. These are proposed diagnostics rather than validated claims about any particular atlas. None of them establishes that the reference metric itself captures cell identity or biological function.

What to try and what to read next

Start by naming the geometric quantity you need. If the task is approximate distances on a fixed set, a data-independent sketch can be useful without learning a principal subspace. Reproduce the three-point example, then repeat it with several independent maps to see the difference between an unbiased squared distance and a reliable individual draw. For real data, keep the same map across points, select a distortion criterion tied to the task, and compare against exact distances before trusting the resulting neighbors. [R1]

Reading list

  • [R1] Primary reading: Database-friendly random projections: Johnson-Lindenstrauss with binary coins. Dimitris Achlioptas (2003). Read Theorem 1.1 for the normalized sign map and the finite point-set guarantee. Compare the dense sign construction and the sparse alternative, which has a specific entry distribution.

  • [R2] Related reading: Extensions of Lipschitz mappings into a Hilbert space. William B. Johnson and Joram Lindenstrauss (1984). Read the finite-set embedding statement in its original context. The broader paper concerns extending Lipschitz maps, not a recipe for biological dimensionality reduction.

  • [R3] Related reading: An elementary proof of a theorem of Johnson and Lindenstrauss. Sanjoy Dasgupta and Anupam Gupta (2002). Read the concentration argument and the move from one difference vector to all pairs. It analyzes a random subspace, whereas Achlioptas supplies the sign-matrix construction.

  • [R4] Related reading: SHARP: hyperfast and accurate processing of single-cell RNA-seq data via ensemble random projection. Shibiao Wan, Junil Kim and Kyoung Jae Won (2020). Read the random-projection and clustering sections separately. The end-to-end benchmark tests a pipeline, not the sign map in isolation.
